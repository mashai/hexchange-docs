# Tarot

Second system in the build order (see [scope.md](scope.md)) — the payoff for one of the two starting
short-term goals in Progression, alongside the cauldron (see [potions.md](potions.md)).

---

## Cards

- Collected from books, never sold individually — see Books in [game-overview.md](game-overview.md).
- Every card belongs to a **source deck** — see Source Decks below.
- Collecting a full matching-source set can take a while, so a functional deck can be assembled from
  mixed sources, but a deck built entirely from one source sells for more.

## Source Decks

Decks are picked by **aesthetic** first, so every player can find one that feels like theirs — some
dark, some minimal, some over the top. **Nine decks in total**, shown in the Grimoire as a **3×3
grid**. Three are public domain (Rider-Waite-Smith, the sister's Marseille, Sola Busca); the other
six are filled by **commissioning an artist** or **licensing an existing deck**.

| Style | Feel | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Classic** | Rider-Waite-Smith — readable, familiar | Public domain. Baseline deck; the demo's main deck. |
| **The sister's deck** | Tarot de Marseille — flat woodcut, bold colors, minimal | Public domain (verify per scan). Story deck — see The Sister's Deck below. |
| **Strange / ancient** | Sola Busca (c. 1490) — alchemical imagery, odd named warriors, unsettling | Public domain (verify per scan). See note below. |
| **Minimal** | Modern line art, mono/duotone, lots of negative space — distinct from Marseille's woodcut | License or commission |
| **Dark / gothic** | Bones, moths, candlelight, unsettling | License or commission |
| **Opulent** | Gold, ornate frames, maximalist | License or commission |
| **Folk / botanical** | Breton folklore, forest, fae, herbs — the game's own setting | Commission (Hexchange-specific) |
| **Soft / cozy** | Pastel, gentle, cute | License or commission |
| **The Traveler's Deck** | The darkest — tied to the story and the bargain. Endgame deck. | Commission (must be original) |

- **Launch with the three public-domain decks plus 1–2 licensed ones** (5–6 decks); add the rest
  after launch. The 3×3 grid shows all nine from the start, including ones not yet unlocked, so later
  additions read as promises kept.
- **~700 cards in total.** Decks mix freely, so nobody needs them all to play well — completing one
  deck is the goal; all nine is for completionists.
- **Sola Busca** is the oldest surviving fully illustrated 78-card deck, and Pamela Colman Smith based
  several Rider-Waite-Smith minor arcana on it (see Pamela Colman Smith below) — a player who owns
  both can spot cards echoing each other across 400 years. Free art, and a look nothing else in the
  lineup has.
- **Full-deck size is per deck, in data** — not hardcoded to 78 — so nonstandard decks (and modded
  ones) work.
- **No Thoth deck** — it's still under copyright.
- **Licensing existing decks doubles as marketing**: indie tarot artists have their own audiences,
  which overlap heavily with Hexchange's. Each licensed deck is a co-announcement.
- **License checklist** for any licensed deck: platforms, term, promotional use (trailer, store
  page), merchandise; whether the deck's own guidebook meanings are licensed or standard meanings are
  used with its art; and whether the artist is fine with art being extractable from moddable data
  files (or licensed art is kept out of them).
- Commissioning 78 cards is the biggest single art cost in this system — get real quotes. License
  where possible; commission what must be ours (the Traveler's Deck, the folk deck).

### The Lost Cards

Four cards of the 15th-century Visconti-Sforza deck are lost to history: **The Devil, The Tower, the
Three of Swords and the Knight of Coins.** In Hexchange, they turn up in the Traveler's books.

- Only these four exist in-game — not the rest of the Visconti-Sforza deck. Commissioned art in the
  gilded Visconti style (no art for them survives, so there are no scan rights to clear).
- **Extremely rare drops.** Finding one is an event.
- **Ordinary cards otherwise** — decks mix and match freely across sources, so a lost card simply
  slots into any deck.
- **Story hook**: one of the Traveler's names is Old Nick, and one of the lost cards is The Devil. Why
  he has cards history lost is left for the player to wonder about. The missing cards are real
  history, so players who look it up find the story — the kind of discovery that gets shared.
- Possible later link: the lost four turn out to belong to the Traveler's Deck. Not committed.
- **The lost Devil is not a rare drop — it's the Devil from the opening.** The Six of Wands and the
  Devil that float out of the newly built shop are **the sister's Six of Wands** (Tarot de Marseille —
  see The Sister's Deck below) and the **lost Visconti-Sforza Devil**. Two clearly different styles
  in the very first scene; players who look the Devil up find a card lost for ~500 years. Nothing in the game
  explains it — it's a wink, not a reveal. The Grimoire shows it under Visconti-Sforza with the other
  three lost cards as locked silhouettes. The Tower, the Three of Swords and the Knight of Coins stay
  extremely rare drops.

### The Sister's Deck

Before she left, the player's sister did a **one-card reading**: *what happens to my sister if I make
this deal?* She drew **the Six of Wands** — triumph — and went. (Story side in
[player-backstory.md](player-backstory.md).)

- **The Six of Wands from the opening is that card.** The player reads it as the shop's triumph on
  opening day — which it is. Only when they finally talk to their sister do they learn it was her
  answer about them, and what let her go.
- **Her deck is the Tarot de Marseille** — a French deck a Breton family would plausibly own, from
  the 17th–18th century so it sits naturally in the near-medieval setting (unlike the Traveler's
  anachronistic stock), and public domain (verify per scan). Its flat, minimal style contrasts with
  the gilded Visconti Devil beside it.
- **The player does not start with her deck** — only that one card. A full deck from the start would
  leave books nothing to add but different art for cards already owned. The Grimoire shows her deck
  at **1/78**.
- **Rebuilding her deck is an emotional collecting goal.** It's a printed edition, so collecting the
  same edition means rebuilding her deck card by card — copies, not her actual cards, so nothing
  needs explaining. The game never says any of this; the player notices the matching style.
- **How it reached the Traveler:** the sister made her initial deal with him and gave him the card to
  pass on — her way of telling her sister to find her, since she can't reach out herself.
- **It's how the player summons her.** Burning it calls the sister — it works by contact (it was
  hers), where the Devil works by likeness. Only this exact card works. A burned calling card goes to
  whoever it calls, so the card goes to the sister: if the conversation goes well she gives it back
  and the player can call her again; if the player leaves angry, she keeps it and that one
  conversation is all there is. Blocked until the player knows her sister is in the Other Realm.
  See The Summoning and the Revelation in [player-backstory.md](player-backstory.md).

### The Virtue Cards

The **Cary-Yale Visconti** deck (c. 1440s, a sibling of the Visconti-Sforza) has cards no standard
tarot deck has: **Faith, Hope and Charity** — the three theological virtues.

- Only these three exist in-game, as ultra-rare extras. Like the lost cards, they're ordinary cards
  in that they mix into any deck — they just fit no standard 78-card slot.
- Original art survives (Yale's Beinecke Library) — verify scan terms before use.
- **Hope** suits a game about looking for someone. Worth considering for story-question pools in
  self-readings (see Self-Readings below) — but as an owned card only, like everything else.
- Open: how a non-standard card behaves in spreads and deck completion (it shouldn't count toward
  any deck's "full" size).

### Pamela Colman Smith

The Rider-Waite-Smith deck was painted entirely by **Pamela Colman Smith**, paid a flat fee, and
uncredited for decades — which is why it's usually just called "Rider-Waite." Her monogram is on
every card. Several of her minor arcana (the Three of Swords among them) were based on photographs
of the Sola Busca deck exhibited at the British Museum in 1907.

In Hexchange the deck is always credited as **Rider-Waite-Smith**, and she gets a nod in-world — a
book, a line from a customer, or the deck's Grimoire entry. It costs nothing and will mean a lot to
the tarot community.

## Burning the Devil

Late game, once the player has **incantations**, they can realize that **any Devil card can be burned
to summon the Traveler** — from any source deck, including the lost Visconti-Sforza Devil from the
opening. Summoning makes him **a character the player can talk to** — nothing more mechanically.
Romance isn't unlocked by burning; it's available through dialogue choices, like any other
character (see The Traveler After the Ending in [player-backstory.md](player-backstory.md)).

- **Never told outright.** No prompt says "burn the Devil card." An incantation book might describe
  calling a being by burning its likeness or token; the player works out which card that means.
- **Burning destroys the card** — same principle as selling a deck forfeiting its cards. More Devil
  cards come from books, so summoning is repeatable, but each burn costs a card.
- **Burning the opening's Visconti Devil is the real sacrifice**: the rarest card in the game, owned
  since minute one. It's a choice, never a requirement — any Devil works.
- **Burned cards go to whoever they call** — Devil cards to him (and the sister's Six of Wands to her; see The Sister's Deck). A card burned to call the devil doesn't just disappear — it ends up in
  his possession. This is why the Visconti-Sforza Devil is lost to history: someone burned it to
  call him ~500 years ago, and he's had it ever since. **Both cards from the opening were in his
  possession** — the Visconti Devil and the sister's Six of Wands — and he gives them to the player on
  the first day.
- **Fits the backstory** (see [player-backstory.md](player-backstory.md)): fae and demons trade in
  deals and can be called. The Traveler handed the player a Devil card on the first day — in
  hindsight, a calling card, and one that has been used before.
- **Historical grounding** (lore source, framed in-game as rumor and found writings, never fact):
  - In 16th-century Venetian witch trials, women invoked the devil using the Devil card from a tarot
    pack — and the card used had to be **stolen**.
  - Early exorcism manuals (Menghi, *Flagellum daemonum*, 1577) called for drawing the devil's image
    and **burning** it, believing the pain passed to the devil himself. In Hexchange, burning compels
    him to come.
  - Tarot cards were printed in the same workshops as saints' cards; an image was believed to stand
    in for the being it depicted — the in-world reason a card works as a calling card.
  - Women were convicted as *herbera* — witches for the magical use of herbs.
  - Speculation (from the same source) that the four lost Visconti-Sforza cards tell a story: the
    Devil called to bring the Tower down on the Knight of Coins over the heartbreak of the Three of
    Swords.
  - Note: Ria Dimitra's 2006 novel *Visconti Devils* shares the missing-Devil premise. Ideas aren't
    copyrightable, but expect the comparison.
- Timing fits [spells.md](spells.md): Invocation is Mid-Late, Invoke Minor Demon is Late.

## Design Principle: Interpretation, Not Prediction

Tarot is treated the way many modern practitioners treat it — a tool for reflection, not a machine that
predicts outcomes. This is what lets the system be serious enough for players who practice and still a
plain, readable game mechanic for players who don't. Nobody has to believe for it to work.

- **The game never explains a reading.** It shows each card, the card's own meaning, and its meaning in
  the position it landed in — nothing more. No generated narrative, no verdict, and no AI-generated
  readings, ever.
- **Cards are never buffs or damage.** No "Death = bad outcome," no "The Sun = +20% sales." Which card
  comes up never changes what a reading pays.
- **Meanings are real.** Standard meanings sourced from public-domain material — A.E. Waite's *The
  Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910). Licensed decks may bring their own guidebook meanings (see
  Source Decks). Upright and reversed meanings are both present.
- The fantasy world itself has real magic (portals, fae), but the reading mechanic never rules on
  whether real-world tarot "works."

Why this matters commercially: tarot is a large real-world interest (roughly half of 13–25-year-olds
engage with tarot or fortune-telling, per Springtide Research), and the tarot games that flopped on Steam
were mostly standalone reading apps with no game around them. The one tarot success, The Cosmic Wheel
Sisterhood (~159K copies, 93%), wrapped tarot in a full game and treated it with care.

## Card Meanings (Data)

Card meanings live in external, human-readable data files — same rule as all game content (see Player

meaning. Practitioners can mod in their own interpretations without touching code.

## Spreads

A **spread** is a layout of positions, each with its own meaning (Past / Present / Future, Obstacle,
Outcome, etc.). The same card means something different depending on which position it lands in — this
is where the depth of a reading comes from.

- **Found in books**, like cards and spells — another reward type in the book reward pool (see Books in
  [game-overview.md](game-overview.md)).
- **Data-driven**: a spread is a file listing its positions and what each position means. Players can
  mod in new spreads.
- Each spread suits certain **kinds of questions** (love, work, a decision, the year ahead…). This is
  what makes picking a spread a real choice in customer readings — see Readings below.
- Spreads range from small (a single card, a three-card past/present/future) up to large ones like the
  Celtic Cross. Bigger spreads need bigger decks.

## The Divination Table

The **Divination Table** (previously called the "Tarot Table" — standardizing on Divination Table going
forward) is unlocked early — **it no longer requires a full set.** A reading only needs a deck with
enough cards to fill the chosen spread's positions; a full matching deck is a reward for collecting, not
a gate. It **unlocks at 10 tarot cards** (visible in the catalog with progress shown) and then
**costs Soul Shards** — cards drive collecting, the price drives the shop loop.

Bought as furniture, and comes with its **own open sign**. It works like the cashier area: with the
sign on, reading customers come to the table and wait for the player; with it off, nobody queues.

Like the PC or till, it has its own dedicated menu. There, the player can:

- Assemble a deck from owned cards (see Deck Lifecycle below).
- Lay a spread and do **readings** — for customers (earns money for the shop) or for themselves.
- See the deck's state at a glance — e.g. "Rider-Waite 41/78, mixed sources" — so it's always clear why
  a customer reacted the way they did.

An invocation spell (see [spells.md](spells.md)) can summon a helper who performs customer readings
autonomously, same earning effect as the player doing it directly.

## Readings

### Customer Readings

The game doesn't judge interpretation, so the player's choices have to sit around the reading, not
inside it. Two core levers:

1. **Spread fit** — each customer comes with a kind of question. Choosing a spread that suits it is the
   main skill. A good fit pays better and pleases the customer; a poor fit still completes, just for
   less.
2. **Deck taste** — every deck works, and no customer ever refuses a reading. But customers have tastes,
   defined per customer type in data:
   - Common customers (e.g. Villagers) don't care what you read with.
   - Discerning customers (e.g. Arcanists, Druids, some named characters) want a full, single-source
     deck — they tip more for one, or give a small disappointed line without it.
   This makes collecting pay off without locking early players out.

Which cards are drawn never affects payout — only the two levers above. Deck cleansing (a deck draining
energy per reading and needing to be restored) was considered and rejected.

### Self-Readings

The player can do readings for themselves at the table. Instead of a free-form question, they pick
from a **list of questions to ask themselves**. This is where self-readings carry the story: the game
never explains anything, but some questions keep turning up the same cards, and the player draws
their own conclusions — e.g. **the Devil showing up again and again** for one particular question.

**Two kinds of question:**

- **Open questions** — cards are drawn at random from the player's deck, same as any reading.
- **Story questions** — each spread position has its own **pool of candidate cards** (with weights),
  so the draw is shaped but not fixed. Position 1 might pick from [The Devil, The Moon, Five of
  Pentacles], position 2 from another pool, and so on. Enough variation that it doesn't feel scripted;
  enough repetition that a pattern shows.

**All data-driven** — a question file lists the question text, its spread, open vs. story mode, and
for story questions the per-position card pools and weights. Questions can carry **unlock
conditions** (story progress, a relationship stage, a found item), so new questions appear as the
story moves. Writers and modders can add questions without code.

**No reading journal.** Past self-readings aren't logged anywhere — noticing the pattern is the
player's job. No hand-holding.

**Only owned cards.** Every reading, story questions included, draws only from cards in the player's
deck. A card that isn't in the deck never appears (rejected: it's eerie, but players would likely
read it as a bug). If a story question's candidate card isn't in the deck, that position falls back
to the rest of its pool.

**The Devil arrives in the opening.** Because story questions can only use owned cards, the Devil has
to be in the collection early. It comes in the intro: **the Six of Wands and the Devil float out of
the newly built shop together, twirling around each other** — triumph and its price. The player
already knows a deal with the devil was made for the shop; what they don't know is why, or what the
price was. The pairing doesn't reveal anything new — it puts those two open questions into the cards
from minute one, and every later reading where the Devil turns up again builds on that image.

**Still interpretation, not prediction** (see the Design Principle above). Story questions shape
which cards appear; the game still never says what they mean. Open questions stay genuinely random.

The earlier "forecast" idea (a morning spread hinting at the day's customers) is parked — story
questions replace it as the main purpose of self-readings.

## Later Layers (After the Demo)

Not core, but planned weight on top of the two levers:

- **Table atmosphere** — candles, incense, time of day, moon phase. Some spreads are favored at night or
  under a full moon; some customers only want readings after dark. Uses the Furniture & Display System
  and the moon-phase conditions in [creatures.md](creatures.md).
- **Named-character readings as story** — when a full character from [the-village.md](the-village.md)
  asks for a reading, their spread is authored and moves their arc forward. Authoring cost stays small
  because it's only named characters, not every customer.
- **Regulars** — a customer whose spread was a good fit comes back and brings others. Reputation as a
  reader grows separately from the shop's general reputation.

## Deck Lifecycle

- **Assembling** a deck removes the used cards from the loose collection.
- **Disassembling** returns them (reversible, while unsold).
- **Selling** removes the deck and permanently forfeits those specific cards — they can never be
  returned once sold.

## Deck Visuals & Pricing

Every deck shares the same box shape/mesh, with the cover texture and price determined by composition:

- A deck built entirely from one source deck resolves to that source's own price and box art (a
  collector's premium).
- A mixed-source deck resolves to a single generic "Custom Deck" price and texture.

Same shared-mesh-plus-swapped-texture mechanism as book cover variation — just deterministic here
instead of random.

## Grimoire

The Grimoire's **Divination Decks** page is the collection overview — a glance, not a workbench.

- **Left page:** title, illustration, a short in-world description of divination decks.
- **Right page:** the nine source decks as a **3×3 grid** of slots (see Source Decks above).
- **Each slot shows the deck's card back** — one image per deck, cheap to produce, and it lets each
  deck's style read at a glance. Decks with no cards found yet show as an empty/silhouetted slot,
  still named (e.g. "Tarot de Marseille — 0/78"), so the player sees what exists.
- **Hovering a slot** shows a small panel: deck name, cards owned / total, cards missing. Nothing
  more.
- **Full detail lives at the Divination Table's menu**: inspecting each card individually (art,
  upright and reversed meanings, source deck) and building/disassembling decks. The Grimoire tells
  the player *what* they have; the table is where they work with it.
- The sister's deck is labeled only as "Tarot de Marseille" — never as hers.
- **Open:** where the special cards live (the four lost Visconti-Sforza cards, the three Cary-Yale
  virtues) — they don't belong to any of the nine slots.

## Future Idea (Not Designed Now)

Trading individual tarot cards — with customers, either at the pub or in the shop. (Not with Forest
Spirits — that's a separate barter marketplace, see [creatures.md](creatures.md).)

**A tarot card game at the inn.** Tarot began in 15th-century Italy as a trick-taking card game, long
before anyone read fortunes with it — and French Tarot is still widely played in France today. A
round at the village inn/pub (see [the-village.md](the-village.md)) is historically right for a
Breton setting, gives the cards a second life, and could be a social activity with the cast.
Potential mini-game, not designed.

## Open Questions

- How a customer's question is shown to the player (a dialogue line, a category icon, both?).
- The question-kind → spread-fit table: which kinds exist, and how strict fit is.
- Whether a deck can be used for readings while it's also on display for sale.
- The first set of self-reading questions — how many open vs. story questions at launch, and which
  story beats they carry. Needs the story outline (see [player-backstory.md](player-backstory.md)).
- Whether any self-reading questions exist in the demo — e.g. one story question where the Devil
  keeps appearing, as a hook toward the full game.
- Burning the Devil summons the Traveler (decided). Still open: is the first burn where "the
  Traveler is fae/demon" lands? What happens on that first summoning? Does burning the Visconti
  Devil give a different result from burning an ordinary one?
- Were the other three lost cards (Tower, Three of Swords, Knight of Coins) burned in the same
  ritual, and so also his — which would explain why they turn up in his books?
- Can cards the player burns come back — e.g. resurfacing later in the Traveler's stock?
- Could the Traveler's Deck be his collection of burned cards from across the centuries, rather
  than a single original deck?
- How often a story question can be asked (once a day? any time?) — too often and the pattern shows
  too fast; too rarely and players never notice it.
