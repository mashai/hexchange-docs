# Player Backstory

This is the story behind why the player made a bargain with the Traveler (see
[game-overview.md](game-overview.md)) and what they're actually doing with the shop, underneath
the shop management. It's the source material for [the-village.md](the-village.md)'s "Personal
Mystery" — that section covers the reveal *mechanic* (which NPCs ask, what sharing unlocks); this
doc covers what's actually being revealed.

None of this is told to the player upfront. It surfaces gradually, in fragments, through pub
conversation — see the-village.md for how.

---

## The Sister

When the player was young, her older sister disappeared. The player's memories of it are
fragmented and unreliable — she was a child. What she does hold onto, clearly, is that her
sister was taken by something supernatural. Rescuing her sister is the player's defining want,
the thing everything else in the game is in service of.

---

## The Sister's Original Bargain

The sister was in a dangerous situation in her village — facing a future she didn't want,
potentially being married off to an old man, or otherwise trapped in something oppressive. She made
a deal with the Traveler (the demon/fae). **Her priority was making sure her younger sister didn't
suffer the same fate.**

The Traveler offered protection — but the protection required her to leave the human world and stay
hidden in the Other Realm. She accepted. From her side, the price was devastating: she got her
sister's safety, and lost her sister's presence.

**Why she has to stay in the Other Realm.** The Traveler can't just bring her back. The bargain is
built around getting her away from the people and circumstances threatening her. In the Other Realm
she is inaccessible to people from her village, protected from the fate she was escaping, under the
Traveler's protection, and outside the reach of anyone who would control her life. He genuinely
fulfils the bargain — but the separation is a necessary stage, not the intended permanent outcome.

**The Six of Wands.** Before she left, she did a one-card reading: *what happens to my sister if I
make this deal?* She drew **the Six of Wands** — triumph. She gave that card to the Traveler to pass
on: since she can't reach out herself, it's her way of telling her sister to find her. See The
Sister's Reading below.

---

## The Player's Bargain

The player went to the Traveler and asked him to rescue her sister directly. He told her he
couldn't. He's not permitted to say why — this refusal is a significant clue, not a throwaway
line, and it should not be explained or lingered on when it first happens. Its weight only
becomes clear much later.

**He can't simply tell her the truth.** The sister's original bargain prevents him from saying
"Your sister is alive. She's in the Other Realm. Go get her." So the sisters have to arrive at the
reunion indirectly, and the player has to discover the truth herself.

What he offered instead: the means to rescue her sister herself. All the knowledge of the world,
and the worlds beyond, in exchange for running the shop. That's the visible shape of the bargain
from the player's side — the shop isn't the point, it's the price of access to what she actually
wants: a way to find and save her sister.

**Why he gives her the shop — two reasons at once:**

1. **He's fulfilling the sister's original deal.** The player needs to be removed from the
   circumstances that could lead her to the same fate. The shop gives her distance from the
   village, financial independence, protection, knowledge, and power. He can truthfully say he
   protected her.
2. **He wants something from the player.** He uses the opportunity to make a new bargain and gets
   something he wanted anyway. Classic fae behavior: he isn't pretending to help while secretly
   sabotaging — he's helping *and* getting something for himself. Those motivations are compatible.

This sits alongside, and doesn't resolve, the "the price of the bargain is never revealed" line in
game-overview.md's opening narrative. Knowledge-for-shopkeeping is the visible part of the deal.
What he gets underneath it stays unrevealed.

---

## The Traveler's Actual Plan

**The crucial point: he is not trying to keep the sisters apart. His plan from the beginning is to
reunite them.**

The separation is necessary because of the sister's bargain, and because staying in the village
would leave her vulnerable to what she was escaping. So his solution is deliberately indirect:
**hide one sister where her village can't reach her, then turn the other sister into someone who
can.**

1. Take the sister out of danger.
2. Hide her in the Other Realm.
3. Keep the player away from the village and its dangers.
4. Give the player the shop.
5. Give her supernatural knowledge and power.
6. Turn her into a witch.
7. Enable her to interact with the Other Realm.
8. Let her discover the truth herself.
9. Give her the means to summon/reach her sister.
10. Reunite the sisters.

The shop, the player's transformation into a witch, the knowledge, and eventually the ability to
reach the Other Realm are all steps toward this. The transformation isn't incidental to the story —
it's how he fulfils the eventual goal of the sister's bargain: giving the sisters a way to find each
other again. He never broke the original bargain. He simply never told either sister the complete
plan.

## Fae and Demons Are the Same Thing

World-building rule: fae and demons are the same creatures, described through two different
cultural lenses. "Demon" is the Christian framing; "fae" is the older one. Both refer to beings
native to another dimension, who can be called into this world — and who trade in bargains and
favors with the humans who call them.

This isn't common knowledge in-world. Most villagers treat fae and demons as separate folklore
categories (consistent with how they're already used as two distinct, both-wrong red herrings in
[mystery-crop-field.md](mystery-crop-field.md) — that's the ordinary, uninformed view, and nothing
here contradicts it). That they're the same thing is a conclusion the player has to arrive at
through her own research, not something any character just tells her.

## The Investigation

For much of the game, the player believes: *something took my sister.* Through books, the Grimoire
and bestiary knowledge, she considers and rules out supernatural explanations one at a time —
ogres, witches, trolls, fae, demons, other entities.

In parallel, her own memory of the disappearance surfaces in pieces, unlocked through pub
conversation as specific NPCs get close enough to ask real questions (the mechanic for this is
spec'd in the-village.md's Personal Mystery section — this doc is the content that mechanic is
drip-feeding).

The two threads — external research and internal memory — converge. What she has to come to
understand:

1. her sister is not dead;
2. her sister is in the Other Realm;
3. her sister went there because of a bargain;
4. she can potentially reach her;
5. she can summon her.

**The first major emotional reversal:** the premise of her investigation was wrong. Nobody kidnapped
her sister. Her sister chose to enter the Other Realm.

---

## The Summoning and the Revelation

Once the player realizes her sister is in the Other Realm, she can eventually summon/reach her — the
payoff of the Traveler's long-term plan.

**She summons her by burning the Six of Wands.** The two opening cards work by the two classic laws of
sympathetic magic:

- **The Devil card works by likeness** — an image stands in for the being it depicts. Any Devil card
  calls the Traveler.
- **The Six of Wands works by contact** — something a person handled stays connected to them. The
  sister drew it, held it, and gave it away; it's the last thing of hers. Only *that* card works —
  another Six of Wands, even from the same Marseille edition, is just a copy.

So the sister's "find me" was literal: the card was the way to reach her all along. There's only one,
and burning it takes it from the player.

**A burned calling card goes to whoever it calls.** Devil cards go to the Traveler (that's how he came
to hold the Visconti Devil); the Six of Wands goes to the sister. So whether the player ever talks to
her again depends on how the conversation goes:

- **If the conversation goes well** — the player stays for the full revelation — **the sister gives
  the card back** at the end. The card that carried "find me" now carries "call me again." Further
  conversations are possible.
- **If the player leaves angry**, the sister never gets the chance. The card stays with her in the
  Other Realm, and **that one conversation is all the player gets.** A deliberately sad ending,
  chosen by the player's own anger.

- The ritual only works once the player knows her sister is in the Other Realm — you can't call
  someone if you don't know where they are. Burning the card before then does nothing (or the game
  doesn't allow it), so it can't be wasted by experimenting.
- The card is still "the proof" in the revelation — through the sister's explanation of what she
  drew and why, not because the player is holding it. It's already burned by then.
- This makes the choice to end the conversation early much heavier: walk away angry, and there's no
  second chance — not even through the Traveler, who doesn't have the card.

**She doesn't know the full truth yet, and she is furious.** *I've spent all these years looking for
you. I've been trying to save you. You left me.* The sister appears to have abandoned her.

**The player can end the conversation here.** If they do, the player's emotional conclusion is
essentially: *my sister chose to leave me behind.*

**If they continue**, the sister reveals the missing part of the bargain. She didn't leave because
she stopped caring. She accepted because she was told her sister's safety was the price — she
knowingly sacrificed their relationship to protect her.

**The card is the proof.** The player carried the Six of Wands from the first day until she burned it
to call her sister. The sister explains it: the card she drew when she made her choice — what her decision would mean for her younger
sister. The physical connection between them across the years and across the worlds. The player
realizes her sister knew exactly what she was giving up, and accepted it because she believed it was
the only way to keep her safe.

**The final realization about the Traveler:** both sisters were manipulated by him — but not to keep
them apart. He was constructing the circumstances needed to bring them back together. The person
she thought was responsible for separating them made their reunion possible — in the most fae way
possible: he fulfilled one bargain, created another, hid the truth, and let the sisters walk
themselves into the reunion.

The sisters' realization isn't *"he tricked us."* It's *"he tricked both of us, but he was trying to
bring us back together."*

## The Sister After the Reunion

If the conversation went well and she gave the card back, the sister becomes **a full character the
player can chat with** — summoned by burning the Six of Wands, which she returns each time. Full
dialogue and relationship like any other character in
[npc-dialogue-and-relationships.md](npc-dialogue-and-relationships.md).

She's **having a great time in the fae world** — and she's **marrying a fae**. The person the player
spent years trying to rescue turns out not to need rescuing at all: she's happy, settled, and full
of stories from the other side. It's a warm counterweight to the Traveler's ambiguity — whatever he
is, things turned out well for her.

---

## Endgame Structure

**The reunion is the narrative ending.** Once the player reaches her sister and the truth of the
original bargain is out, the main arc is complete. No further major plot escalation is needed.

**The story is finished, but the game is not over.** The shop stays playable: run and improve it,
discover remaining knowledge, collect, experiment with systems, progress further, pursue optional
relationships and content.

---

## The Traveler After the Ending

He stays present after the sister storyline resolves. **The game never resolves his moral nature.**
The player should be left unsure whether he's genuinely kind, genuinely selfish, both, manipulating
everyone, helping because he cares, helping because it benefits him — or operating by a supernatural
morality that doesn't fit human ideas of either.

**Evidence he's kind:** he protected the sister, kept her safe in the Other Realm, protected the
player from the same fate, gave her the shop, money, independence and knowledge, helped make her a
witch capable of reaching the Other Realm, and created the circumstances for the reunion.

**Evidence he's selfish:** the player's shop and transformation are tied to a new bargain that gives
him something he wanted. He withholds information, uses technicalities, lets the player
misunderstand for a long time, engineers rather than explains, and gets her to pay a price for
something he was already intending to do.

**Neither becomes canon.** No "secretly good all along," no "only helped because he wanted
something." He never gives an explanation that resolves it. If accused of manipulating her, he can
acknowledge it without apologizing — he did manipulate her; that doesn't mean he lied about caring.

**His philosophy:** fae logic, not human morality. "I helped you" and "I manipulated you" can both be
true. "I care about you" and "I benefited from what I did to you" can both be true.

### Romance

Burning a Devil card summons the Traveler as a character the player can talk to (see The Devil Card
below). Summoning doesn't unlock romance — **romance is an option through dialogue choices, the
same as for any other character** (see [npc-dialogue-and-relationships.md](npc-dialogue-and-relationships.md)).
The player can trust him, flirt with him, romance him, challenge him, accuse him,
question his motives, try to understand his morality, or stay suspicious. These choices change the
relationship without ever resolving who he really is — the ambiguity is part of the romance. The
player can be drawn to someone she knows manipulated her, while unsure whether his feelings are
genuine.

### Intended final feeling

The player understands *what* he did, but never *why*. The final question isn't "is he good or
evil?" It's: *did he help us because he cared, because it benefited him, or because those two things
were never separate for him?* The game doesn't answer it.

## The Sister's Reading

The card side of The Sister's Original Bargain above (tarot details in [tarot.md](tarot.md)):

- The Six of Wands floats out of the player's new shop on the first day (see The Opening in
  [demo.md](demo.md)). The player reads it as the shop's triumph — which it is.
- Its real meaning — what the sister's decision would mean for the player — is revealed in the
  summoning conversation, and only if the player stays for the full revelation.
- On the first day, the Traveler isn't just handing over a gift; he's delivering the sister's
  message.
- Her deck was the Tarot de Marseille. The player only has this one card from it; rebuilding the
  same edition is a quiet collecting thread (see The Sister's Deck in tarot.md).

## The Devil Card

A thread that runs the length of the game (details in [tarot.md](tarot.md)):

1. **Opening** — the lost Visconti-Sforza Devil floats out of the new shop beside the Six of Wands.
   The player knows a deal with the devil paid for the shop; not why, or the price.
2. **Mid-game** — the Devil keeps turning up in self-reading story questions.
3. **Late game** — with incantations, the player realizes any Devil card can be burned to summon
   the Traveler. The card from the first day was a calling card.
4. **After that** — burning a Devil card summons him as a character to talk to. Any relationship,
   romance included, comes from dialogue choices like any other character.

Burned calling cards go to whoever they call — Devil cards to him — which is why the Visconti Devil
vanished from history (burned ~500 years
ago to call him) and why he had it to give. Both opening cards were in his possession.
