# Breton Folklore — Forest Creatures

Per-species reinterpretation pass for the Forest Creatures group in [creatures.md](creatures.md), following
the decision to ground the setting more deeply in Brittany and Celtic folklore. For each of the eleven
current Forest Creatures: what fantasy tradition the existing name actually comes from, whether Brittany
has a real equivalent, and — where it does — the folklore detail that becomes gameplay material (a taboo,
a rule, a reward/punishment condition).

Scope: mythological identity and grounding only. Rarity, spawn conditions, and exact preferences are a
separate, still-open pass — see creatures.md's Per-Species Condition Details. Renaming the underlying
`type` identifiers in the data files is also out of scope here — see What This Doesn't Cover, below.

---

## The Portal Rule

The shop sits in one specific culture, but the Forest Portal — and the Otherworld beyond it — connects it
to the rest of the world. A creature with no real Breton root isn't a mistake to fix; it's a customer, a
spell, or a piece of knowledge that arrived from elsewhere, same as a witch from a different setting could
one day step through this shop's own portal. Four of the eleven current Forest Creatures fall into this
category.

## Reinterpreted

### Banshee → Kannerezed Noz (the Washerwomen of the Night)

Three old women seen at riverbanks, pools, and lakes past midnight, washing the burial shrouds of whoever
in the parish is about to die. The washing itself is the omen — nothing else needs to happen for the
debt to be paid.

**The Rule:** they call out to passersby for help wringing the cloth. Helping traps the helper in the
work itself, and death follows. Walking away without answering is the only way through. A book brought in
from elsewhere may describe surviving by matching their wringing rhythm instead — the Kannerezed Noz's own
account doesn't recognize that as ever having worked. Worth keeping both versions on the shelf rather than
picking one as correct.

**In the Grimoire:** Church framing treats them as a curse or a haunting. Druidic sources treat them as
older than the Church's framing entirely — women serving a debt, not evil spirits.

### Werewolf → Bisclavret

Brittany's actual werewolf tradition, not a reskin of one — a Breton lai collected by Marie de France in
the 12th century. Unlike curse- or bite-based werewolf lore, Bisclavret's condition is a private, ongoing
arrangement: he becomes a wolf for stretches of days, and needs a specific object — his clothing, in the
original — hidden somewhere safe to change back. Losing access to it, not the transformation itself, is
what traps him.

**The Rule:** the wolf's temperament isn't fixed by the fact of being a werewolf. Bisclavret's tale is
explicit that the beast and the man aren't the same nature — he behaves nobly even while stuck as a wolf.

**In the Grimoire:** Folk accounts treat every werewolf as a monster on sight. The Bisclavret story argues
against that directly — a book that complicates what Folk Knowledge already told the player, rather than
confirming it.

### Goblins → Korrigan

Brittany's general term for its fairy folk, most often translated into English as "goblin," "elf," or
"gnome" depending on the source. Beautiful by moonlight, withered and red-eyed by day — which is why
they're rarely seen during business hours. Drawn to water: fountains, wells, rivers. Known for taking
what isn't theirs, historically framed as stealing infants and leaving a changeling in the cradle — the
folklore basis for a species whose gameplay role is theft.

**The Rule:** a Korrigan can't recite all seven days of the week without stumbling — the week is sacred
and they have no place in it. It's the reliable way to catch one out. They also can't stand churches,
priests, or anything Marian, a holdover from having opposed Brittany's conversion outright.

**In the Grimoire:** the clearest example of the split the whole knowledge-web idea is built on. Church
books call Korriganed devils in disguise. Druidic sources call them the old people of the land, here
before the Church was. Neither side is lying — they're describing the same thing through incompatible
frames.

### Kobolds → Korred

Small — two to three feet — and far stronger than the size suggests; local telling credits them with
hauling the standing stones of Carnac into place by hand. They dance in circles around dolmens and stone
rows every night and pull in anyone who wanders too close.

**The Rule:** a captive pulled into a Korred dance is expected to add the next line to their song —
usually the next day of the week in sequence. Get it right and they reward the captive, sometimes lifting
a curse from a previous unlucky guest; get it wrong, or run out the week, and whatever affliction that
last guest was carrying becomes theirs instead.

**What changes from "Kobold":** the household-spirit role doesn't carry over. Korred aren't hearth pests —
they're outdoor, tied to megaliths, encountered at stone circles rather than in a cellar. That's a real
shift in where and how this species would need to be encountered, not just a rename.

### Bogfolk → Groac'h

A solitary fae living in underwater caves, beneath beaches, or in marsh pools. Multiform — old crone,
beautiful stranger, whichever suits the moment. Genuinely double-edged: treated with respect, she's
hospitable, offering treasure, cures, and knowledge of what's hidden underground; cross her — she takes
offense easily — and the one surviving tale about her ends with the fae herself transformed into "the
queen of mushrooms."

**The Rule:** not an automatic threat. Closer to a moody landlord than a monster — how she's treated
decides which side of her the visitor gets.

**Open note:** the source material is one specific named fae, not a race. Bogfolk as a customer species
assumes there's more than one of her — a deliberate expansion of the source, not a direct transplant, the
same way Ogres or Cyclops would be if they stayed in the roster.

## Adapted (loose fit — flagged, not a clean transplant)

### Mushroom Folk

No standalone Breton creature answers to "mushroom folk" directly. The one concrete Breton anchor is the
Groac'h tale above — crossed, she's turned into "the queen of mushrooms." That's not a species, but it's
a real, specific image to build from rather than inventing one outright: Mushroom Folk as remnants or
attendants of a Groac'h's domain, not an unrelated fae race that happens to also live in this world.

Wider Celtic-adjacent folklore, not Breton-specific, also consistently ties mushrooms to fairy rings —
circles where the fae dance, mark territory, or open a way between worlds. That pattern is common enough
across Celtic-influenced traditions generally to lean on without claiming it's uniquely Breton.

**Open Question:** whether Mushroom Folk stay their own species loosely tied to Groac'h lore, or get
folded into Groac'h/Bogfolk as one connected family instead of three separate customer types. Worth
deciding before writing Bestiary text for any of the three.

### Satyr → Bugul Noz

Satyr is Greek — lecherous, musical, half-goat — and nothing in Breton tradition answers to that specific
combination. The closest match in role (solitary, forest-edge, encountered at dusk) is the Bugul Noz, the
"night shepherd." The fit is only partial: Bugul Noz is described as hideous and is feared for how he
looks, not for anything he does — several accounts say he was, if anything, protective, discouraging
people from wandering unsafe ground after dark by being frightening rather than by doing harm.

**The Rule:** he avoids crossroads — can't tolerate the shape of a cross. Otherwise leaves people alone
unless they ignore his warning to head home.

This changes what the customer is about, not just its name. Satyr as written trades on charm and
mischief; Bugul Noz trades on being misjudged. If the goal is a Breton-authentic version rather than a
reskin with a new label, that shift should be treated as the point, not a compromise.

## Not Native — Portal-Brought

None of these four have a real foothold in Breton tradition:

- **Ents** aren't folklore at all. The word and the concept are Tolkien's — an Old English word for
  "giant," repurposed for tree-shepherds. There's no source tradition underneath it to reinterpret.
- **Ogres** come out of French literary fairy tale (Perrault and descendants), not oral Breton tradition.
  Brittany's own megalith folklore explicitly credits its standing stones to fairies carrying them in
  their aprons, not giants — giant-built-the-monument stories are a German/Continental pattern that
  doesn't show up in the Breton material.
- **Cyclops** is Greek, full stop.
- **Vampire** is Slavic/Balkan in origin. Breton death-and-omen folklore runs through different figures
  entirely — Ankou, the Kannerezed Noz above — rather than anything resembling a reanimated-corpse
  vampire.

Per the Portal Rule above, that's not a problem to fix — it's the shop's own worldbuilding doing exactly
what it's supposed to do. Each of these can carry an explicit "not local" marker in the Grimoire: a Druid
or villager dismissing the idea outright ("A vampire? Here? Those are eastern stories"), followed by a
Traveler-brought book, or a witch passing through the portal, as the actual source of what the player
knows about it.

**Open Question:** who specifically brought each one in — a named traveling witch, a category of book,
the Traveler himself — isn't decided. Doesn't need to be decided now, but each of these four should
eventually have a named source in its Bestiary entry rather than staying unattributed, since "brought by
someone" is the entire point of keeping them in the roster.

## What This Doesn't Cover

- Rarity, spawn conditions, and exact preferences per species — still fully open, per creatures.md's
  Per-Species Condition Details.
- Renaming the underlying `type` identifiers in the data files. The Breton names above (Kannerezed Noz,
  Korrigan, Korred, Groac'h, Bisclavret, Bugul Noz) are what the in-world Grimoire and NPCs would call
  these creatures — the existing English labels can stay as the internal species identifiers without
  contradiction, the same way a real animal has both a common name and a different name in another
  language. Whether to actually rename the identifiers in code/data is a separate, later call.
- Tarot, spell, or Bestiary text implementing any of this — this is reference material for that pass, not
  the pass itself.

## Open Questions

- Mushroom Folk's relationship to Groac'h/Bogfolk — one family or three separate species (see above).
- Named sources for the four not-native species, once the Bestiary text for them gets written.
- Whether High Fae & Demons (Imp, Succubus, Demon, Nephilim) get a comparable pass. They weren't in scope
  here — they're already framed as Church/demonological terminology for Otherworld beings per
  player-backstory.md, which may or may not need the same treatment.
