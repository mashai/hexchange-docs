# The Village

*[Name TBD]*

A small village. Everyone knows everyone. The kind of place where news travels faster than people,
and what you do on Tuesday is being discussed by Thursday. It sits somewhere between fantasy-medieval
and actual medieval — which means women still deal with the same things they always have,
and sometimes a witch is the only person they can go to.

The witch shop is a known quantity. People have opinions. Most of those opinions are complicated.

---

## The Cast

### Clarisse
Young woman. Always in the corner of the pub with a book. Obsessed with vampire fiction —
specifically Carmilla, which she'll tell you is a gothic novella from 1872 and not a romance novel,
thank you very much. She wants to meet a real vampire. Friendly when she likes you, biting when she doesn't.
Friendship only — she's not here for the player, she's here for vampires.
*See: clarisse/*

---

### Emery — The Gossiper
The village gossip and a tremendous flirt — well-liked because he actually pays attention to people,
not because he's trying to be liked. Last son of a village family — no land or career to inherit.
Lends a hand in the fields, makes himself useful, gets by. Not bitter about it — freed him from the
path everyone else in his family took.

Sleeps around, openly, and never thinks twice about it. If he's in a romance with the player and
they ask for exclusivity, he'll go along.

Wants desperately to travel. Hasn't yet. Talks to everyone partly because he cares,
partly because they're the world until he gets to see it.

Intrigued by the player from the start. One of the characters who can draw out the player's
backstory — he asks the kind of questions that make you want to answer them.
Open to romance, fully — no conditions, no gating arc.
*See: emery/*

---

### Maren — The Disapprover
Proper, composed, performing respectability at considerable personal cost. Publicly disapproves
of the witch shop — loudly, if it comes up. Comes in through the side entrance when nobody's looking.

Comes to the shop for a love potion — not to coerce a stranger, but to bring her husband back to
the gentle man she married. She hints, more than states, that he's not gentle with her anymore.
Left to her own devices she doesn't question the marriage itself — the player just listening keeps
her coming back, but it takes the player actively pushing back on her framing to move her toward
leaving.

Her arc ends, if the player pushes her there, with her leaving her husband. Quietly. On her own terms.
The player can have one-sided flirty dialogue options during her arc — she won't register them.
Romance only becomes available after her arc is complete and she has left her husband.
*See: maren/*

---

### Aldric — The Old Man
Lives in the village, usually found at the pub nursing a lukewarm beer. Has children and
grandchildren who all live in the village. Mostly keeps to himself and people leave him to it,
although he's respected by most.

Secretly a werewolf — proud of it, never shown in-game, and never registers with the player's
bestiary through him alone. Once the player has actually encountered a werewolf elsewhere and has
built up enough quiet, no-agenda familiarity with him, he tests them on what they know about local
werewolves before trusting them with anything real.

His grandson doesn't want to become a werewolf, and told Aldric so directly — Aldric's the only one
who knows, and it's kept from the rest of the family. He brings it to the player at the pub and asks
what a witch might be able to do. The player can help with a brewed potion, or choose not to
interfere and tell him it's not something to change — both are valid, and the friendship survives
either one.

Not open to romance.
*See: aldric/*

---

### Wren — The Open One
Simply, visibly fine with the witch shop. Not making a point about it — just unbothered,
and genuinely pleased there's a witch in the village. Warm, funny, immediately likeable.
Labrador energy. Happy to see you, happy to help, no agenda.

Tells the player not to worry about Maren. Cheerfully. Already moving on.

Occasionally says something surprisingly perceptive buried inside something cheerful
and moves on before you can register it.

Open to romance — straightforward about it, same as everything else. Will not tolerate
flirting with others if in a romance. Make an enemy of her and there are no second chances,
no explanation given — she'll just be somewhere else.
*See: wren/*

---

### Rook — The Hunter *(traveller)*

Known to the village as "the hunter" — not by name. Passes through. Kills things that need killing.
Practical, unsentimental about the supernatural — it's a job. Confident, sarcastic, genuinely
respectful. Free spirit with no interest in roots.

Not looking for love. Would do anything for the right person without making a speech about it.
Has bestiary knowledge the player needs and shares it once trust is established.

Rare appearance. When they're in the village the gossiper knows first.
Kills dangerous creatures — the player can ignore it, agree it has to be done, or push back
and try to change their mind. They listen. They don't always agree. The player's stance shapes
the relationship and creates friction with Clarisse's vampire hopes and the trader's contacts.
Their relationship with the trader is complicated — respect and friction in equal measure.
Open to romance. They will leave eventually. That's part of the deal.
*See: hunter/*

---

### Rowan — The Trader *(traveller, nonbinary)*
Known to the village as "the trader" — not by name, same as the hunter. Trades with supernatural
creatures rather than killing them. Witch mother, unknown father — grew up between worlds, never
quite fit either, chose the one that asked less of them to perform being normal. Guarded, precise,
transactional at first — not cold, just someone who's stopped expecting warmth to go well.

What's underneath is unexpectedly deep: cares genuinely about every creature they deal with — human,
forest creature, fae, demon alike, no ranking between them. The player has to earn seeing any of it.
Information is currency; fair exchange is respected. Knows their bestiary contacts as individuals,
not categories.

The player's shop stirs something connected to their mother. They won't bring it up.

Rare appearance. When both travellers are in town something shifts in the room.
Their history with the hunter involves real tension and real trust in equal measure.
Open to romance — slowly, on their own timeline.
*See: trader/*

## Dynamics

- **The gossiper** is the social connective tissue. He knows everyone and talks to everyone.
  The player will learn things through him — sometimes useful, sometimes just texture.
- **The hunter and trader** are a pair in the background. Opposite approaches, same world.
  The player being close to one probably affects the other.
- **The disapprover** and **the open one** are the two faces of the village's relationship
  to the shop — the player sees both and can engage with both.
- **Clarisse** and **the old man** are the pub's atmosphere. Different kinds of self-contained.
- **The travellers** are rare. When they appear, it matters.

## The Shop in the Village

The witch shop isn't neutral. Running it means the player has a reputation, whether they want one or not.
Some people come in secretly. Some pretend they haven't been. Some are openly curious.
Some are hostile until they need something.

Women especially. The kinds of things they might need a witch for — quietly, without their husbands knowing,
without the village knowing — those things don't get said out loud. But they come to the shop.

That context should live in the background of conversations. It doesn't need to be stated directly.
It's just true.

## LGBT & Romance

This village is mostly fine with it — not as a political statement, just as how things are.
Nobody makes it a thing. The gossiper flirts regardless of gender. Clarisse's obsession with vampires
isn't framed around gender. The travellers' dynamic isn't notable for being what it is.

The game supports romance with any character who's open to it, without the player's gender
affecting what's available. No one in the village comments on it. It's just life.

There's room for one character who carries some quiet weight from having grown up in a harder time —
not as conflict or a lesson, just as texture. But sad LGBT stories are not the default here.
The point is that this is a place where people can just exist.

## The Pub

The pub is where the player wants to be. Not because they have to go — because the gossiper heard
something good, because Clarisse is three drinks in and talking about Carmilla again, because the
trader just got back and the player has questions. It should feel like a reward for a day in the shop.

Most conversations are warm, funny, or quietly meaningful. Romantic tension if the player wants it.
Friends who are genuinely glad to see you. The odd piece of gossip that turns out to be useful.

The difficult interactions live mostly in the shop — someone comes in, the player makes a call, life
goes on. They don't follow the player to the pub. The pub is lighter than that, and it needs to stay
that way for the heavy moments elsewhere to have any weight.

## Difficult Requests

The shop will receive requests that touch on harder things — a love potion, help with an abusive ex,
an unwanted pregnancy. These are not edge cases. They're part of what a village witch actually was for.

In every case, the player decides how to respond. There is no correct answer built into the game.

- Turn them away entirely
- Ask questions, push back, make them think
- Help without comment

All three are valid. All three are the player's call. The game does not moralize.

The people making these requests are not issues to resolve — they're people with a situation.
They should be written as such. The love potion request in particular needs care:
the person asking might be desperate, naive, or not fully understanding what they're asking for.
The player reads them and decides.

The tone is restraint. No dramatic music. No judgment from the game. Just a person, a request,
and the player's choice.

### The unwanted pregnancy

She comes to the shop because there is nowhere else to go. The interaction is written to show the reality
of her situation — not to lecture, but to make it visible. A player who has never had to think about this
might find themselves thinking about it.

If the player refuses to help: she has the baby. She dies in childbirth. The game doesn't frame this as
punishment for the wrong choice. It's just what happens when someone in that position has no help.
The player who chose not to help has to sit with that. The player who helped doesn't get a trophy.

### The love potion

Three paths, all available to the player:

- **Redirect** — help them understand what they're actually feeling. They came in wanting to coerce someone
  and leave having understood something about themselves. The hardest path to write, the most interesting one.
- **Enable** — give them what they asked for. Consequences follow in the world.
- **Call it out** — tell them directly what they're asking for is not okay. They can leave, argue, or
  have the conversation.

The person asking isn't written as a villain. They might be desperate, naive, or not fully
understanding what a love potion means for the other person. The player reads them and decides.

## Transient Characters

Not everyone in the village has a full arc. Some people appear once or twice — someone who needed advice
on a breakup, someone passing through with a problem. They get what they need (or don't) and move on.
No obligation to return. They make the world feel like it keeps moving whether the player is watching or not.

## Mysteries

### The Personal Mystery
The player is here to find her sister — taken, as far as she remembers, by something supernatural
when they were both children. Rescuing her is what everything else in the game serves. Running the
shop is the price of the knowledge that might get her there, not the point of it. None of this is
stated upfront. It comes out slowly, through conversation, as certain NPCs get close enough to start
asking real questions.

The player doesn't choose their backstory. It already exists. What the player chooses is whether to share it.

Sharing unlocks more — more dialogue options, more of the story visible. Keeping it close is also valid.
The answers are visible in the dialogue options, so the player discovers who they are at the same moment
they decide whether to say it out loud.

Not every character earns these questions. The ones who do ask differently based on who they are.
Clarisse doesn't judge — which makes her a natural confidant despite not being the obvious choice.
The gossiper notices things. The travellers have seen enough of the world to recognise what someone
running toward something looks like.

This section is the reveal mechanic — which NPCs ask, what sharing unlocks. What's actually being
revealed (the bargain with the Traveler, what really happened to her sister, and the fae/demon
truth underneath it) is spec'd in full in player-backstory.md.

### The Crop Field Mystery
Something is wrong with the crops. The village is unsettled. It turns out to be the neighbouring village —
entirely human, entirely mundane, no supernatural explanation. Just neighbours being awful to each other.

The contrast is intentional. In a world where vampires are real and the witch shop is open for business,
the thing that's actually threatening the village is ordinary human pettiness. Quietly funny, a bit sad.
